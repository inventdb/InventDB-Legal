Build your five core reports

You don’t write any code, any SQL or any formulas. You type a sentence in plain English, check the answer, then tell it to save that answer as a report and email it to you on a schedule. This guide gives you the exact words to type, where to type them, and — most usefully — the numbers you should get back, so you can tell straight away whether it worked.

Before you start

Two minutes of orientation. Everything here is already set up on your account — you only need to know where things live.

The three screens you’ll use

  • Analyze — where you ask questions and where reports get created.
  • Reports — where saved reports live. Opens “Report Studio”.
  • Workflows — scheduled jobs, and the inbox of things waiting on you.
  • Dashboard, Records, Files and Import aren’t needed for this.

How every one of these five works

The rhythm is identical each time, so it is worth reading once rather than five times:

  • Ask in Analyze. Plain English. A short “thinking” timeline runs, then a table appears.
  • Check it against the numbers in this guide. If they match, the question was understood. If they don’t, this guide tells you what to say to correct it.
  • Click a row. It should open the actual matter or invoice. If it opens the wrong thing, the answer is joined wrongly — say so and it will fix it.
  • Say “turn this into a report”. A report card appears in the conversation.
  • Say “create a workflow” to have it email itself to you on a schedule. Rehearse it in the sandbox before you activate it.
Everything in Analyze is read-only. You cannot break anything by asking. It never edits a matter, never sends an email and never moves money — changes only ever arrive as a proposal you approve. Ask badly, get a wrong table, ask again.

What you’re building

The five reports, in the order you should build them

  1. What’s due this week
    The docket. The one report the firm cannot operate without. Expect: 44 deadlines, 15 Critical, 0 overdue.
  2. Who’s expected in court — and who can’t be in two places
    Diary clashes, before they become non-appearances. Expect: 32 events, 7 clashes.
  3. What I can bill right now
    Unbilled time, by attorney, with its age. Expect: $300,640 across 773.3 hours.
  4. Who owes me money
    The named chase list, not the ageing chart. Expect: 470 invoices, $778,109.
  5. Is the client trust account clean
    The one that has to be right. Expect: $1,268,901.63 across 258 matters, 0 negatives.
Build them in this order. The first two are the fastest wins and the easiest to sanity-check. By the time you reach the trust account you’ll recognise the rhythm, which matters, because that one is the longest and the only one you will ever have to defend to an auditor.
Report one

What’s due this week

Every open deadline falling due in the next seven days, grouped by whoever owns it, worst first — plus anything already past its date, however far back.

What this tells you Whether anything is about to be missed while there is still time to stop it. For a litigation practice this is the single most valuable half-page in the business — it turns “I think something’s due on the Orozco matter” into a named list with a statute cited next to each line.

Report five

Is the client trust account clean

The balance, every matter holding client money, and the one exception that matters more than all the others put together.

What this tells you Whether the firm can honestly answer an audit question today. California requires the trust bank balance, the firm’s books and the sum of every individual client ledger to agree. A single client ledger showing a negative balance means one client’s money paid another client’s costs — and that is where a State Bar audit starts.
1

Ask for the exception first, not the balance

Screen: Analyze · new conversation
Start a fresh conversation, paste this, press Ask.
AnalyzeAsk
Look at the client trust ledger and find any matter whose running client ledger balance has ever gone below zero. For each one show the matter, the client, the date, the transaction that took it negative, and the balance at that point. If there aren't any, say so clearly.
It should come back and tell you plainly that there are none.
Zero is the right answer, and it is the answer you should get. Across all 5,755 trust transactions and 976 matters, no client ledger has ever gone negative. This report runs every day anyway — its entire value is the morning it comes back with a row on it.

One that can only be reached one way is a number you are trusting on faith — and this is the one report where that is not good enough.

Freeze it, don’t keep it live

Screen: Report Studio
Open the report and save it as a snapshot rather than leaving it Live.
This is the one report that must not be live. A live report re-runs its questions every time somebody opens it. That is exactly right for the other four and exactly wrong here — next month’s numbers would silently replace the figures a partner signed. A signed reconciliation is evidence; freeze it, and keep the file in Files against the trust record, where re-uploading a corrected version keeps the old one rather than discarding it.

Screen: Analyze → Workflows
Two workflows, not one — a daily exception check and a monthly statement.
Analyze · scheduleSend
Create a workflow: every morning at 7am, every day including weekends, check for any client trust ledger with a negative balance. If there are none, email me a one-line note saying so. Send the empty one too. A report that only arrives when something is wrong is indistinguishable from a report that has quietly stopped running. One line a day saying “258 matters, no negative ledgers” is how you know the check is alive.
The monthly one cannot finish on its own, by design. It stops and waits for a signature, and the bill run is told to wait for the release note. You cannot apply trust money to an invoice from a ledger nobody has proved.

Once all five are running

Four habits that keep them useful. Reports rot faster than anyone expects — usually silently, because a question that returns no rows is not an error.

  • Give every workflow a named owner, not a role and not a distribution list. An unowned approval is an approval nobody presses.
  • Let only two things interrupt a person — a negative trust ledger, and a practising certificate about to lapse. Everything else waits for its slot. Add a third alarm and all three start being ignored.
  • Keep sending the empty ones. Silence is ambiguous: it means either all-clear, or the workflow broke three weeks ago.
  • Once a quarter, read one rehearsal run of each workflow end to end. Check the row counts against the numbers in this guide. Data drifts, and a filter that matched everything in March can match nothing in September without anyone being told.
Then keep going. These five are the ones every litigation practice needs. The reporting catalogue lists forty-one more that this data will support — and ten that it won’t, with the reason for each.
